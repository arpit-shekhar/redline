# 0005. Leverage is stored with the user's red lines

Redline asks the user once whether they can walk away from this deal, stores the
answer alongside their red lines, and uses it to tone every counter-offer — from
"I require" to "would you consider."

The counter-offer is the capability that separates Redline from free tools that
merely explain a contract. But whether a counter-offer helps depends entirely on
something the document cannot reveal: whether the user can afford to lose the
deal. A freelancer sending marked-up terms to their largest client is risking
the relationship that pays their rent. A tenant in a tight market who returns an
edited lease gets replaced by the next applicant. A well-drafted counter-offer
handed to someone with no leverage is not neutral — it can cost them the thing
they were trying to get.

The research asks whether anyone actually sends the counter-offer or just signs
with a bad feeling (open question 3), but never connects that to leverage.

Leverage belongs with red lines rather than as a per-document question because
it is a standing fact about the user, not a property of the document.

## Considered options

- **Ignore leverage; draft every counter-offer identically.** Rejected: the
  wording that helps someone who can walk away is the wording that costs the job
  for someone who cannot.
- **Draft a firm and a soft version of every counter-offer.** Rejected: doubles
  the output for every flag.
- **Drop counter-offers from version 1 and only explain risks.** Rejected: this
  removes the only capability that distinguishes Redline from the free
  alternatives already in this segment.

## Consequences

- The product asks a question before it delivers anything, which is friction at
  the moment a first-time user is deciding whether to bother.
- Leverage is self-reported. Users routinely misjudge their own position, and
  the analysis has no way to check the answer.
