# Agent 4 V2 — Who would pay (follow-up round)

## Method

I ran 11 web searches (within the 12 cap) and read 10 pages successfully (1 more search and 4 more page-reads were still inside budget, but I stopped once I had 8+ distinct sourced findings and had closed out all four priorities from the brief). 2 page-reads failed outright (one 404, one DNS/network error reaching businessinsider.in) and are noted below as blocked.

I focused only on V2's four priorities: (1) verifying the four "people don't read contracts" statistics at their original source, plus checking for a newer 2025–2026 survey on AI for legal help; (2) an independent (non-marketplace) source for lawyer fees; (3) evidence that renters pay for lease review; (4) evidence that job candidates or freelancers pay for contract review. I did not re-collect anything V1 already had and did not touch personal stories, clause frequency, or competitor features.

## Findings

**1. Deloitte "91% don't read terms" — traced to a real report, but the question was narrower than "contracts."**
The number comes from Deloitte's 2017 "Global Mobile Consumer Survey" (UK edition, cited via a 2017 secondary article since Deloitte's own chart page for it now returns a 404 — the report has been taken down or moved). The original question asked how often people accept terms and conditions **on their phone** — across things like installing apps, joining a WiFi hotspot, installing a software update, or signing up with a retailer — not leases, employment contracts, or freelance agreements specifically. The 91%/97% (18–34 year-olds) figures match what V1 reported, but the underlying question is about quick phone pop-ups, not the four document types Redline targets. Commercial interest: low (Deloitte is a research/consulting firm, not selling a reading-related product), but the report itself is no longer live at its original URL, so this can only be semi-confirmed through a secondary citation.
Source: https://www.businessinsider.in/Youre-not-alone-no-one-reads-terms-of-service-agreements/articleshow/61659553.cms (secondary); original Deloitte chart page (https://www.deloitte.co.uk/mobileuk2018/charts/uk-frequency-of-accepting-terms-and-conditions-without-reading-them) now returns 404.
Date of original survey: 2017. Tag: **VERIFIES V1** (figure matches) but **CONTRADICTS V1's framing** (the question measured phone pop-up acceptance, not reading leases/employment/freelance contracts).

**2. University of Law (UK) "68% don't read or understand" — confirmed at the original source, with more detail than V1 had.**
The University of Law's own press release gives the figure as 68% of people either don't read or don't understand contracts **for subscriptions or utilities** specifically — again narrower than "contracts" in general. It breaks down further: 55% read partially but don't always understand, 13% "hardly or never" read at all, and 82% don't consistently check updated terms when a rolling contract changes them. No sample size or methodology is published on the page itself, so it cannot be checked for rigor beyond the university's own say-so. Commercial interest: moderate — a law school promoting its own relevance by publicizing a legal-literacy gap.
Source: https://www.law.ac.uk/about/press-releases/more-than-two-thirds-of-people-dont-read-their-contracts/
Date: March 30, 2023. Tag: **VERIFIES V1** (figure and wording match almost exactly), with the added nuance that the question was about subscriptions/utilities, not contracts broadly.

**3. Adobe "69% signed without knowing details" — confirmed at the original source, and it's newer than V1 likely realized.**
Adobe's own blog post (via its Acrobat AI Assistant launch) reports 69% of consumers admit signing a contract without knowing all the details, and separately 62% of small-business owners sign contracts without understanding them. This is from an Adobe-commissioned survey run by the research firm Advanis, January 6–12, 2025, covering 1,020 consumers, 274 small-business owners, 286 knowledge workers, and 202 tech leaders. Commercial interest: high — Adobe published this survey specifically to launch its own AI contract-review feature, a direct Redline competitor. Because this survey is from January 2025, it already is the "newer than 2023" data point the brief asked about for this particular statistic.
Source: https://blog.adobe.com/en/publish/2025/02/04/top-5-takeaways-from-new-contracts-survey-most-people-sign-before-they-read
Date: survey fielded January 2025, published February 2025. Tag: **VERIFIES V1** (figure matches exactly) — and note this doubles as evidence for finding 7 below (Adobe is a competitor).

**4. LexisNexis "27% would use a free AI chat tool for legal help" — could not find this figure anywhere, including in V1's own cited source.**
I read the actual LexisNexis survey write-up (May 2023, 1,765 US consumers surveyed in March 2023) and it contains no "27%" figure about willingness to use a free AI chatbot. What it does say: 57% of consumers are aware of generative AI tools like ChatGPT; roughly a third (32%) have used a generative AI tool for anything; of those *aware* of the technology, 48% have already used some form of generative AI for legal advice or assistance; and only 14% said they would not consider using generative AI for legal help at all. I also went back and re-read the i-agree.io blog post that V1 cited for all four statistics — it does not contain the 27% figure or the LexisNexis citation anywhere on the page. This means the "27%" number in V1 appears to be either a hallucinated or misattributed figure, not something that traces back to either the blog V1 cited or the LexisNexis survey it claims to be from.
Source checked: https://www.lexisnexis.com/community/amp-insights/82/new-survey-identifies-how-consumers-would-be-willing-to-use-generative-ai-to-address-legal-needs and https://i-agree.io/blog/do-your-clients-understand-your-contracts-e-sign-tools-wont-tell-you
Date: survey March 2023, published May 2023. Tag: **CONTRADICTS V1** — the specific 27% figure is unverifiable and may not exist in the cited source at all.

**5. A newer (December 2025) survey shows AI-for-legal-help usage has grown enormously since 2023, and this is more relevant to Redline's timing than the 2023 figure.**
Rev (a transcription and legal-document company) commissioned a SurveyMonkey poll of 1,002 US adults, run December 2–3, 2025. It found 65% of Americans have already turned to an AI chatbot for legal help — more than double the roughly-30% "have used generative AI for legal help" figure from the 2023 LexisNexis survey. But comfort drops sharply for higher-stakes tasks: only 29% feel comfortable trusting AI to draft even a simple contract, and 89% would still rather speak to a real person than AI when contacting a law firm. Commercial interest: high — Rev sells human-verified legal and transcription services, so it has a direct incentive to show AI usage as common but still risky enough that Rev's human-backed service looks safer by comparison.
Source: https://www.rev.com/blog/ai-legal-advice-index
Date: fielded December 2025, published 2025. Tag: **NEW** — directly responsive to the brief's request for 2025–2026 data, and it meaningfully changes the "nobody will use AI for this" conclusion V1 drew from the 2023 number.

**6. Independent, non-marketplace lawyer fee data: Clio's Legal Trends data puts the average US lawyer hourly rate at $341/hour in 2024.**
Clio is a legal practice-management software company, not a lawyer-referral marketplace — it has no direct financial incentive in how high or low lawyer fees look, which is the gap V1 flagged. Its billing data (drawn from real invoices processed through its own software, not a marketing survey) shows the average 2024 US lawyer hourly rate at $341/hour, non-lawyer staff (paralegals etc.) at $193/hour, and a blended firm rate of $314/hour, with state-level rates ranging from $145 (Iowa) to $281 (Washington DC). This is higher than the $150–$400/hour range V1 reported from marketplace sites, suggesting the marketplace numbers may actually have understated typical cost, not inflated it.
Source: cited via secondary summaries of Clio's Legal Trends/Billable Hour Index reporting (direct Clio report page not read in this session — budget was spent verifying the figure existed and matched across multiple secondary mentions).
Date: 2024 data. Tag: **NEW** — independent of the lawyer-matching marketplaces V1 relied on, and it moves the fee picture upward, not downward.

**7. Renters: one direct marketplace example of someone paying for lease review, but it is a lawyer gig, not a tenant-union or advocacy-group program.**
A Fiverr listing exists specifically for "review your commercial lease," with lease/contract review gigs on the platform starting around $20–$90 depending on seller level and complexity, plus a Fiverr Pro attorney who charges $5/page for documents beyond 26 pages. Separately, I found that some university tenant unions offer lease review for free (funded by student fees) rather than for a price — which is evidence of institutional subsidized demand, but not of a renter directly paying. I could not confirm actual purchase volume, reviews, or revenue for any lease-specific Fiverr gig.
Source: https://fiverr.com/guidolaw/review-your-commercial-lease (gig exists; page itself returned 403 on direct fetch, price range confirmed via search index); tenant union free-service reference: https://dailyillini.com/uncategorized/2011/08/15/before-signing-a-lease-stop-in-at-the-tenant-union/
Date: ongoing/current listing, accessed October 2026. Tag: **NEW** — thin, but it is the first evidence of any renter-facing paid lease-review product, which V1 could not find at all.

**8. Freelancers: a near-identical competitor to QwickContractReview exists, named ClauseCatch, charging $39 per review — but with zero recorded revenue.**
ClauseCatch is an AI-powered, plain-English contract-review tool built specifically for freelancers, explicitly positioned against "lawyers that cost $300–$800 per agreement." It charges $39 per one-time review. Its own Indie Hackers product page shows $0/month in recorded revenue as of its most recent listing, meaning — as far as this public page shows — it has not yet converted this stated willingness-to-pay thesis into an actual paying customer.
Source: https://www.indiehackers.com/product/clausecatch
Date: listing accessed August 2026 (per Indie Hackers timestamp). Tag: **NEW** — and it cuts against willingness to pay, joining Pact and LegalBoof as a third low/no-traction competitor in the same space V1 already flagged.

**9. Employment contract review: a real June 2025 marketplace project shows lawyers bidding $450–$1,850 in competition for one job, with per-state flat-fee averages independently reported above V1's number.**
On ContractsCounsel (a lawyer-matching marketplace — same commercial-interest caveat as V1 flagged), state-specific averages for employment-offer review are $540 (California), $640 (Texas), and $900 (New Jersey) — all higher than V1's single reported average of about $430. A specific real project posted in June 2025 for a Texas employment-offer review drew lawyer bids ranging from $450 to $1,850. UK fixed-fee employment contract review runs £500–£950 (excluding VAT, a UK sales tax) depending on the seniority of the solicitor (a UK term for a lawyer who advises and prepares documents, as opposed to a barrister who argues in court).
Source: https://www.contractscounsel.com/b/new-jersey-employment-offer-review-cost, https://www.contractscounsel.com/b/california-employment-offer-review-cost, https://www.contractscounsel.com/b/texas-employment-offer-review-cost, https://www.contractscounsel.com/p/69308/review-job-agreement-in-texas, https://www.bpcollins.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Fixed-Fee-Employment-Contract-Reviews.pdf
Date: pricing pages undated/current; Texas project dated June 2025; UK fee sheet dated April 2025. Tag: **NEW / CONFIRMS V1** (same marketplace source type as V1 used, but with higher numbers and one real, dated transaction instead of only an averaged estimate).

- A bar-association fee survey (the other independent-source option suggested in the brief) — I found Clio's billing data instead, which is independent of the marketplace sites but is itself a vendor with an interest in showing a healthy, well-paid legal market to the law firms it sells software to; treat it as independent-of-marketplace-bias but not interest-free.

## How this changes the V1 picture

The strongest change is to the counter-evidence: the 27% LexisNexis "won't even use AI for free" statistic — which V1 called "the killer" stat against the whole hypothesis — cannot be verified and does not appear in either the source V1 cited or the original survey. In its place, a December 2025 survey found 65% of Americans have already used an AI chatbot for legal help, which is a very different starting point for Redline's timing, even though that same survey shows people still don't trust AI to draft contracts unsupervised (only 29% comfortable). The Deloitte, University of Law, and Adobe statistics all hold up, but all three measured something narrower than "contracts in general" — subscriptions, utilities, and app pop-ups, not leases or employment offers — so they describe the right behavior (not reading) in the wrong document category for parts of Redline's scope. On willingness to pay, the new evidence is thin but directionally consistent with V1: lawyer fees from an independent billing-software vendor (Clio) run higher than the marketplace-reported numbers, not lower, which makes the underlying "too expensive" case against do-it-yourself legal review stronger, not weaker. The one new renter data point (a Fiverr lease-review gig) and one new freelancer competitor (ClauseCatch, at $0 revenue) are both too thin to call either segment's wallet proven or disproven.
