# Redline: research summary, round 2

This is the second round of research. Four new researchers each re-read their round-1 notes, then went after the gaps round 1 left. Each one had the same limits as round 1: at most 12 web searches and 15 pages read, and a source link on every finding.

Round-2 notes: `agent-1-who-has-this-pain-v2.md`, `agent-2-what-goes-wrong-v2.md`, `agent-3-what-already-exists-v2.md`, `agent-4-who-would-pay-v2.md`.
Round-1 notes and synthesis: the same file names without `-v2`, and `summary.md`.

This file replaces `summary.md` where the two disagree. The biggest correction is in section 5: one of round 1's main arguments against the product rests on a number nobody can find.

---

## Verdict up front

**The problem is still real. The case against building it got weaker. The case for building it did not get stronger.**

Round 1 gave three reasons for doubt. Here is where each one stands now.

1. **"Nobody is asking for help before they sign."** Weaker, not gone. Round 2 found one person asking for help with an unsigned lease, and one person who asked a question at the signing table. That is two cases across two rounds of searching. The usual pattern is still that people find the painful clause when they try to leave.
2. **"People won't use even a free AI tool for this."** This one fell apart. Round 1's key number, "only 27% would use a free AI tool for legal help," cannot be found in the survey it was credited to. A December 2025 survey says 65% of Americans have already used an AI chatbot for legal help. People are using these tools, but mostly free general chatbots.
3. **"This already exists and nobody uses it."** Stronger. Round 1 found two consumer apps with almost no users. Round 2 found five more aimed at the same people, all launched or announced in the last year. One of them shows $0 in monthly revenue. None shows traction.

So the question has moved. Round 1 asked "will anyone use this?" The evidence now says yes, people already paste contracts into free chatbots. The new question is **why anyone would pay for Redline instead of using ChatGPT for free.** Round 2 found one concrete answer, in section 3: chatbots quietly change contract wording, and Redline's rule of always quoting the exact sentence guards against that.

## 1. The three sharpest pain points

### Pain point 1: The final contract was not the one I read

A London tenant read a draft lease by email. It said she could leave with one month's notice. At the signing appointment, the paper lease said two months. She asked whether it matched the draft and was told it did.

> "I appreciate that I signed the document that states 2 months, but I did question when signing, if it was the same as the contract they had sent me via email, and they told me it was exactly the same. I feel this change should have been pointed out. If I had known anything was different about it, I would have sat in their office for as long as it took and read every word, as I had done on the one they had emailed me."

Source: Harringay Online community forum, "cara howard," May 7, 2013. https://harringayonline.com/xn/detail/844301:Comment:488593

**Why it is the sharpest:** it is the first lease story either round found. It is also the only case where someone tried to check a contract at the exact moment of signing. She had already read it once, so her problem was not confusing wording. She needed to know what had changed between the draft and the final copy. She found out when she tried to leave, like everyone else in the evidence.

### Pain point 2: The expensive clause is found at the exit

This is round 1's main finding, and round 2 found nothing that contradicts it.

> "Disgusted to find out that It will cost me £95 to cancel a subscription to premier pro cc, which I have never used. It was not immediately apparent when I signed up and hidden in the t&c. I would never have signed up if I knew i just wanted to see what the sofware was like."

Source: Adobe Community forum, March 11, 2019. https://community.adobe.com/t5/download-install-discussions/i-feel-scammed-by-contract-complaint-about-cc-early-cancellation-fee/td-p/10423998

### Pain point 3: Someone spots a bad clause before signing, but cannot tell if it is legal

This is the pre-signature moment round 1 could not find.

> "the new contract they are wanting her to sign has a special added clause stating she can be issued a section 21 after a few months into the contract, i know the renters Bill changes in may 2026, but this means she could be issued a section 21 if she was to sign this contract before that bill becomes law."

Source: MoneySavingExpert forum, "katiem188," November 15, 2025. https://forums.moneysavingexpert.com/discussion/6640315/tenancy-advice

A "section 21" is an English eviction notice that lets a landlord end a tenancy without giving a reason. A new law was about to ban it. The landlord added a clause timed to get in before the ban.

**Why it matters:** the poster had already found the risky clause on their own. What they wanted to know was whether it was legal under a law that was about to change. Section 5 explains why that is a problem for Redline as currently designed.

Round 1's freelancer quote ("I lost my house due to not getting paid") is still the most painful single story on file. It stays out of the top three because round 1 already showed Redline can't fix non-payment.

## 2. The clause types that matter most, ranked

The top eight are round 1's ranking. Nothing found in round 2 had stronger evidence. The numbers are not comparable with each other: some count complaints, some are survey results, some are dollar totals. The order is a judgment call.

| # | Clause | What it does, in plain words | Evidence | Round-2 change |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Weak freelance payment terms | The contract doesn't force the client to pay on time. | 71% of freelancers have had trouble getting paid (survey of 5,358). | None |
| 2 | Security deposit withholding | The landlord keeps some or all of the deposit at move-out. | Only 42% of renters who moved got their full deposit back. | None |
| 3 | Automatic renewal | The contract renews and keeps charging you unless you cancel by a deadline. | 100,000+ complaints to the US Federal Trade Commission (FTC, the main US consumer-protection regulator) over five years. More than 90 a day in 2025. | The FTC's "click-to-cancel" rule, which would have made cancelling easier, is now fully struck down by a federal appeals court. The FTC reopened work on a new rule in March 2026. No federal rule is in force. |
| 4 | Personal guarantees | The owner personally repays a business debt if the business can't. | 59% of small firms with debt used one. | None |
| 5 | Late fees | An extra charge on top of what you owe. | $14 billion (US dollars) in credit-card late fees in 2022. | None |
| 6 | Non-competes | Stops you working for a competitor after you leave. | About 18% of US workers, roughly 30 million people. | The FTC's ban was struck down and removed from the federal rulebook on February 12, 2026. Only state laws limit non-competes now: 9 states plus Washington, D.C. |
| 7 | Forced arbitration | You give up the right to sue in public court. | 2,393 complaints to the US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau in 2025, up 150% since 2023. | None |
| 8 | Indemnity and liability caps | You agree to cover the other side's losses, or they limit what they could ever owe you. | Round 1 had no numbers. Round 2 found one court dataset: about 93,000 US federal insurance-coverage lawsuits from 2009 to 2017. | That dataset covers disputes with insurance companies, not indemnity clauses between two ordinary parties. It is the closest count that exists, but it is indirect. |

Seven clause types were added in round 2. All of them are real and often discussed. Only one has any count at all.

| Clause | What it does | Evidence found |
|---|---|---|
| Ownership of your work (intellectual property assignment) | Says who owns what a freelancer makes. | One claim that it causes "60-70%" of freelance disputes. The source page was blocked and nothing else repeats the number. Treat it as unverified. |
| Kill fees | A fee owed to a freelancer if the client cancels partway. | Industry convention only (25-50% of the remaining value). No count. |
| Data and privacy terms | What a company may do with your personal data. | One 2019 survey: 71% rarely read terms of service. Nothing newer. |
| Unlimited revisions | The client can demand endless free rework. | Anecdotes on Upwork and Fiverr forums. No count. |
| One-sided termination | Only one side may end the contract early. | Law-firm opinion only. |
| Exclusivity | Stops you dealing with competitors. | Individual lawsuits only. |
| Built-in price increases | Lets the provider raise the price mid-contract. | No US data. Ireland announced plans in November 2025 to regulate these clauses in phone and broadband contracts. |

**What round 2 settled:** for these seven, the answer isn't "not researched yet" any more. Round 2 looked, and no regulator or survey seems to track them as categories. That means Redline can't rank them by measured harm, only by opinion.

**One inference, not a finding:** two of the top eight clauses (automatic renewal and non-competes) lost their federal protection during 2025 and 2026. That leaves people with fewer outside protections, so understanding these clauses before signing may matter more. No source in either round measured this.

## 3. Where the existing tools are weak

### The real competitor is a free chatbot

People paste contracts into ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini. A trade group for contract professionals published two concrete errors from general chatbots:
- A chatbot "corrected" a company's legal name inside a signed contract from "Exhibiton" to "Exhibition."
- Asked to copy a liability clause "without modifying the wording," it changed "acts of negligence or omissions" to "acts of omissions or commissions." The new wording covers any action, not just careless mistakes.

Source: World Commerce & Contracting. https://www.worldcc.com/resource/can-you-trust-generative-ai-contract-co-pilots-not-quite-but-a-new-type-of-autopilot-might-change-this.html

**This is the strongest new point in favor of Redline's design.** Redline's rule is that every flag quotes the exact sentence it came from. The documented chatbot failure is quietly changing the exact sentence. Because the gap is specific and checkable, it can be shown to a buyer.

### Quoting the exact sentence is possible, but not unique

- **GC.ai** already sells "exact quote" as a named feature: click a citation and see the original passage highlighted. It sells to corporate legal teams, not individuals. https://gc.ai/features/exact-quote
- **Spellbook** answers questions with links to the relevant clause. This came from a search summary, not a page the researcher opened. Whether its risk flags also quote the exact sentence is unconfirmed.
- **LegalOn** and **Rocket Lawyer's free Copilot** are still unconfirmed after two rounds.

**What this means:** no consumer tool was confirmed to quote the exact sentence. That makes exact quotes a possible difference for Redline, not a proven one.

### The consumer field is crowded and nobody is winning

| Product | Aimed at | Price | Traction evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pact (round 1) | Renters, freelancers | About $1 per contract | 2 App Store ratings. Why it stalled is still unknown. |
| LegalBoof (round 1) | Freelancers, renters, small business | Not checked | Too few reviews to show a score. |
| Rocket Lawyer Copilot | Small business | Free (confirmed in round 2) | Large established brand. |
| ClauseCatch | Freelancers | $39 per review | $0 monthly revenue on its Indie Hackers page (a website where small software founders post their numbers). |
| AELA.AI | Freelancers | Not published | Already drafts counter-offers, the same as Redline's feature 3. No reviews found. |
| VIDI | Founders, freelancers, small business | Not published | Launched February 2026. 9 upvotes on its March 2026 relaunch. |
| ReviewMyContract | Freelancers, small business, individuals | Free | Launched November 2025. Deletes files after analysis. |
| BeforeJD | Freelancers, consultants, small business | Not published | Was planned for April 2026. Whether it launched is not shown. |

Sources for each are in `agent-3-what-already-exists-v2.md` and `agent-4-who-would-pay-v2.md`.

**The pattern:** at least seven small products target Redline's exact audience. Two of them are free, and a free chatbot sits under all of them. None has visible traction. Building this kind of product looks easy. Getting people to use it does not.

**Still unchecked after two rounds:** why Pact stalled; Luminance, Lexion, ContractPodAi, DocJuris, Pactum; two more new names (FlagMyContract, Signoti).

## 4. Who would plausibly pay, and roughly what

The order is the same as round 1. Round 2 added prices but no new proof that anyone in these groups actually pays.

**1. Small business owners and freelancers.** Round 1's evidence still stands: a $99 (US dollars) flat-fee review service, and legal plans at $49-169 a month. Round 2 added a counter-signal: ClauseCatch charges $39 per review to freelancers and shows $0 revenue. Rocket Lawyer gives a version away free.

**2. Job candidates reviewing an offer.** Lawyer prices are higher than round 1 reported. Flat fees average $540 (California), $640 (Texas) and $900 (New Jersey). One real Texas job posting from June 2025 drew lawyer bids from $450 to $1,850. In the UK, a fixed-fee review costs £500-950. These come from a site that profits from matching people to lawyers, so treat them as industry estimates.

**3. Renters.** The first sign of renters paying anything: lease-review services on Fiverr, a freelance marketplace, cost about $20-90. The number of buyers is unknown. Some university tenant unions review leases for free.

**4. Creators reviewing brand deals.** No change from round 1: $800-1,400 per review from a niche law firm.

**What a lawyer costs, from a source that doesn't sell lawyers:** Clio makes billing software for law firms. Its data puts the average US lawyer rate at $341 an hour in 2024. Two caveats:
- The researcher took this from second-hand write-ups and did not open Clio's report.
- The same notes list state averages of $145-281, which cannot produce a $341 national average.

Read $341 as "around $300 an hour" until someone checks the original.

**Rough read:** round 1's range of $10-99 per document is still the defensible band. Its low end now has free competitors. Its high end, $39, has a competitor making no money. No one in either round said in their own words what they would pay.

## 5. What contradicts your hypothesis

**1. Correction to round 1: the "killer" number is gone.** Round 1 called "only 27% would use a free AI tool for legal help" the most damaging fact against the product. The round-2 researcher read the LexisNexis survey it was credited to, and the number isn't there. It isn't in the blog round 1 cited either. The survey says only 14% would refuse to consider AI for legal help. **Remove the 27% figure from anything built on round 1.**

**2. The other three "nobody reads" numbers are real but narrower than round 1 said.**
- **91% accept terms without reading (Deloitte).** The question was about phone pop-ups such as app installs and Wi-Fi sign-ins.
- **68% don't read or don't understand (University of Law).** The question was about subscription and utility contracts.
- **69% signed without knowing the details (Adobe).** This one does cover contracts. Adobe ran it in January 2025 to launch its own AI contract-review feature.

None of the three measured leases, job offers or freelance agreements.

**3. People use AI for this already, so Redline's real rival is free.** A December 2025 survey of 1,002 US adults found 65% had used an AI chatbot for legal help. Only 29% would trust AI to draft even a simple contract. Rev, which sells human legal transcription, paid for the survey and benefits from both findings. https://www.rev.com/blog/ai-legal-advice-index

**4. The two people found at the signing moment wanted things Redline does not do.** Redline reads one document and answers only from that document. Neither person needed that:
- The tenant who asked at the signing table already understood her lease. She needed to compare two versions of the same contract, the draft and the final.
- The person with the unsigned lease had already found the bad clause. They needed to know if it was legal under a law about to change. Redline's rule to "state only what the document says" stops it from answering that.

That's two people, which is too few to design around. But it is all the pre-signature evidence on file, and neither case matches the product as written.

**5. The market is crowded with products that have no users.** Seven consumer tools exist, two of them free, and none shows traction (section 3). Round 1 said building this is not the hard part. Round 2 found more evidence for that.

**6. Freelance agreements still have no first-person story.** Two rounds tried and failed. Freelance agreements are one of the four document types Redline names, and freelancers are its strongest paying segment on paper. The stories that turned up were freelancers writing their own contracts, not reviewing a client's.

**What cuts in your favor:**
- Chatbots demonstrably change contract wording. Redline's exact-quote rule targets that precise failure.
- Most people are already willing to use AI for legal help.
- Federal protections on automatic renewal and non-competes have weakened, so people are more on their own.
- Lawyer prices are higher than round 1 thought, which widens the gap between a lawyer and a tool.

## 6. How far to trust this round

- **Few new stories.** Agent 1 found three new stories, not eight. Two of them are about one lease each, and the third is a relative's account. All three are from UK forums. Reddit, Avvo, BiggerPockets and Law Stack Exchange were blocked or returned nothing, so the lease evidence is UK-only and the clause and price evidence is mostly US.
- **Errors in the round-2 notes that I caught while writing this:**
  - Agent 2 says the FTC dropped its non-compete appeal on September 5, 2026, but also that the rule left the rulebook on February 12, 2026. The order is impossible. The appeal date is probably 2025, but the notes don't show which is right.
  - Agent 4 calls the 65% figure "more than double" the 2023 level. The 2023 survey's 32% was people who had used AI for anything, not for legal help, so the two numbers are not comparable.
  - Agent 4's Clio fee numbers don't add up (section 4).
- **Unverified claims, kept out of the conclusions:**
  - A Stanford figure on AI legal errors (69-88%).
  - A general "10-30%" chatbot error rate, quoted second-hand.
  - The "60-70%" freelance-dispute figure.
  - Spellbook's citation behavior.
- **Sources with a commercial interest:**
  - Rev (the 65% survey) sells legal services.
  - Adobe (the 69%) sells an AI contract tool.
  - ContractsCounsel (the employment-offer prices) makes money matching people to lawyers.
  - Clio sells software to law firms.

**Net:** the clause data and competitor data are solid. The human evidence is still thin: eight stories across two rounds, most from subscriptions, gyms and UK leases. Round 1 said the missing piece is people, not more searching. Round 2 confirms it: searching public forums has hit its limit.

## 7. Open questions this research could not answer

1. Why would someone pay for Redline when a free chatbot reviews the same contract? Does showing the exact sentence change their answer?
2. When people find a bad clause before signing, do they want it explained, or do they want to know whether it is legal? The one case found wanted the second.
3. How often is the real job comparing a draft with the final version, rather than reading one document?
4. Why did Pact, LegalBoof and ClauseCatch get no traction?
5. Does a freelancer ever review a contract a client sent them, worried? Two rounds of searching found no one doing it.
6. Does anyone send the drafted counter-offer? Unchanged from round 1, still no evidence either way.
