# Agent 2 — What goes wrong

This report answers one question: which contract clause types actually cause the most real-world pain, and how do we know? "Contract clause" here means one written rule inside a document — a lease, a freelance work agreement, a business contract, or a website's terms of service.

## Method (searches run, pages read, when you stopped and why)

- **Web searches run:** 11 (guardrail allowed up to 12).
- **Pages fetched or read in full:** 4 succeeded — a PDF report from the National Association of Consumer Advocates (NACA) on forced arbitration complaints, a PDF report from Freelancers Union on late/non-payment, a statistics page from RapidEye Inspections on security deposits, and search-result summaries used for the remaining points. 6 additional fetch attempts were blocked: the FTC's own website (ftc.gov), the Federal Reserve's small-business survey site (fedsmallbusiness.org), the Economic Policy Institute (epi.org), and the Federal Register all returned "403 Forbidden" errors or bot-blocking redirects when fetched directly. Where a primary government page could not be opened directly, the numbers below are drawn from search-result text of that same government report, or from a secondary source (a law firm or news outlet) that quotes the primary report directly — each is marked below.
- **Stopped when:** 8 distinct, separately-sourced findings were reached, which is the guardrail's stopping point ("more is not better"). This happened after roughly two-thirds of the allowed searches, so the stop was evidence-driven, not budget-driven.
- **What this means for completeness:** several candidate clause types on the original list — kill fees (a payment owed if a freelance project is cancelled early), unlimited-revision clauses, exclusivity clauses, fee escalators, and data/privacy terms — were not reached before the 8-finding stop point. They are listed in "What I could not find" below, not guessed at.

## Ranked clause types

Numbers below come from different kinds of evidence — government complaint counts, national surveys, and one case where only lawyers' professional experience was available (no hard count). They are **not** directly comparable to each other on a single scale. The ranking below is my best-judgment ordering by how often the underlying evidence says people run into the problem, with the type of evidence stated plainly in the last column of the confidence section. Treat the order as a reasonable synthesis, not a scientific ranking.

## Detail on each clause

1. **Freelance non-payment / weak payment-and-ownership terms.** Freelancers Union surveyed 5,358 independent workers in 2015 and found late-or-non-payment is one of the top problems in freelance work: 71% had trouble getting paid at some point in their career, 50% had trouble in the single prior year, and among those, 81% were paid late and 34% were never paid at all for some of the work. The average freelancer who had trouble lost $5,968 in unpaid income in that one year — 13% of their annual income — and the longest anyone waited to be paid averaged 98 days. Only 28% of freelancers always work under a written contract. Real example, quoted directly from the report: *"I lost my house due to not getting paid back in 2010. Two clients, approximately $6,000 total. I learned the hard way it's best to demand partial payment along the way."* — Sonya W., web designer, Texas. Source: https://www.onlabor.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/FU_NonpaymentReport_r3.pdf

2. **Security deposit withholding.** Zillow's 2024 Consumer Housing Trends Report (as compiled by RapidEye Inspections) found that of renters who moved out of a previous rental, only 42% got their entire deposit back; 20% got most of it, 20% got some, and 10% got none at all. 52% of movers disagreed with their landlord or property manager about something at move-out — usually who was responsible for repairs, unpaid utility bills, or move-out cleaning costs. A separate Rent.com survey of 1,000 renters found 26% have lost a deposit entirely at some point, and of those, 36% received no explanation from the landlord for why the money was kept. Source: https://rapideyeinspections.com/research/security-deposit-statistics/

3. **Automatic renewal ("negative option" clauses).** These are subscriptions or memberships that renew and keep charging your card unless you cancel by a specific deadline. The FTC's push for its 2024 "click-to-cancel" rule cited its own complaint system receiving over 100,000 complaints about these practices over five years, with the daily rate climbing from roughly 33–42 complaints a day around 2021 to 70–90 a day by 2024–2025. The FTC describes the recurring pattern: people sign up (often via a "free trial" that quietly becomes a paid plan), then find cancelling difficult or nearly impossible. Note: the "click-to-cancel" rule itself was later struck down in court on procedural grounds and the FTC has since restarted the rulemaking — the underlying complaint numbers are not affected by that legal back-and-forth. Source: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/10/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-click-cancel-rule-making-it-easier-consumers-end-recurring

4. **Personal guarantees.** This is a promise, usually buried in a business loan or commercial lease, that the business owner will personally repay the debt out of their own pocket — including their house, car, or savings — if the business itself can't pay. The Federal Reserve's Small Business Credit Survey (a large annual US survey of small employers) found that among firms carrying debt, 59% had secured it with a personal guarantee, and 38% had pledged personal assets like a home or retirement account. This clause is the main way a "limited liability" business structure — which is supposed to protect an owner's personal assets — gets undone in practice. Source: https://www.fedsmallbusiness.org/reports/survey/2026/2026-report-on-employer-firms (secondary summary: https://www.crestmontcapital.com/blog/federal-reserve-small-business-lending-survey)

5. **Late fees and penalty charges.** The CFPB found that credit-card companies collected over $14 billion in late fees in 2022 alone — more than 10% of all the interest and fees credit-card issuers charged that year. The typical late fee climbed steadily from $23 at the end of 2010 to $32 in 2022. The CFPB's director at the time, Rohit Chopra, said card companies were "exploiting a loophole to harvest billions of dollars in junk fees." The CFPB capped the typical fee at $8, though this action's current legal status was outside this report's scope to verify. Source: https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-bans-excessive-credit-card-late-fees-lowers-typical-fee-from-32-to-8/

6. **Non-compete clauses.** A non-compete stops a worker from taking a job with a competing company, or starting a competing business, for some period of time after they leave. In its 2024 rulemaking to ban most non-competes for workers, the FTC estimated that 18% of the US workforce — about 30 million people — is currently covered by one. This is not limited to executives or people with trade secrets: independent academic estimates (cited by the Economic Policy Institute, a labor-focused research group) put the range as high as 27.8% to 46.5% of workers, or 36–60 million people, depending on how the question is asked. Source: https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/noncompete-rule (academic range: https://www.epi.org/publication/epi-comment-on-the-ftcs-non-compete-clause-rule/)

7. **Forced arbitration / class-action waivers.** These clauses require you to resolve any dispute through a private arbitration process instead of a public court, and usually also ban you from joining a class-action lawsuit (a group lawsuit where many harmed people sue together). NACA (National Association of Consumer Advocates, a lawyers' group) reviewed the 5.6 million complaints the CFPB received in 2025 and found 2,393 that mentioned arbitration — a 9% increase from 2024 and a 150% increase from 2023. Within that set: 152 complaints said the company itself would not cooperate with the arbitration process it required (for example, refusing to pay the arbitration fees), and 107 complaints said the company kept taking harmful action — like reporting a negative mark on the person's credit file — even while an arbitration case was still pending. Real example, quoted from a complaint in the report: *"I financed a vehicle through LENDER that was sold with undisclosed major mechanical failures... LENDER was notified of the fraud but continues to collect payments and report the account. I'm also disputing the enforceability of their arbitration clause under Nevada law."* Source: https://www.consumeradvocates.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/CFPB-Complaints-Fact-Sheet-4-1.pdf

8. **Indemnity and liability-cap clauses.** An indemnity clause means one side agrees ahead of time to pay for the other side's legal costs or losses if a dispute arises — sometimes even when it wasn't clearly their fault. A liability cap means the other side sets, in advance, the maximum amount they could ever be forced to pay you, no matter how large the actual damage turns out to be. No government complaint database or national survey covering these clauses specifically was found in this search. What is available is professional commentary: several business-litigation law firms independently describe indemnity clauses as among the most heavily negotiated terms in any contract, and as a leading source of litigation for small businesses, because their exact scope (what counts as "your fault," whether there's a dollar cap) is often left vague. This is a real, repeated professional observation, but it is not a hard count — treat it as a signal, not a measurement. Sources: https://www.aikenslawfirm.com/the-contract-clauses-that-create-the-most-litigation-for-small-businesses/ ; https://www.gleamlaw.com/blog/business-litigation/indemnification-litigation/

## How confident this ranking is

This ranking mixes three different kinds of evidence, and they are not directly comparable — that is the single most important caveat in this report:

- **Hard regulatory complaint counts** (an actual count of complaints filed with a government body): automatic renewal (100,000+ FTC complaints over five years) and forced arbitration (2,393 CFPB complaints in 2025, trending up sharply). These are real measured counts, but they only capture people who filed a formal complaint — most people who get burned by a bad clause never do that, so these numbers almost certainly undercount the true scale of the problem.
- **National survey prevalence numbers** (a survey asked a sample of people whether something happened to them, then scaled up): freelance non-payment (71% career rate, from a 5,358-person survey), security deposit withholding (58% don't get the full amount back, from Zillow's renter survey), personal guarantees (59% of indebted small firms), and non-competes (18% of the workforce, an FTC estimate). These are strong, credible numbers, but "prevalence" (how common the situation is) is a different measurement than "complaint volume" (how often people report being harmed by it) — a clause can be extremely common without every instance of it causing serious harm.
- **Dollar-scale regulatory findings** (a total dollar figure the government calculated, not a complaint count): late fees ($14 billion collected in one year). This measures financial scale, not the number of people affected.
- **Professional/qualitative signal only, no hard number**: indemnity and liability-cap clauses. This is the weakest evidence tier in this report and is flagged as such — do not read its rank-8 position as "least damaging," only as "least measured" in the time available.

Because these measurement types don't share a common unit, the rank order above is a judgment call, not a statistically derived ranking. A different, equally defensible ranking could be built by, say, sorting only by survey prevalence, or only by dollar-scale harm — those would produce a different order for items 3 through 6 in particular.

## What I could not find

- **Kill fees** (a fee a client owes if they cancel a freelance project partway through): no national survey or complaint data located before the search budget's stopping point was reached.
- **Unlimited-revision clauses** (a freelance contract that lets the client demand endless free reworks): no data located.
- **Exclusivity clauses**: no data located.
- **Fee escalators / price-increase clauses**: no data located.
- **Data and privacy terms** in consumer contracts and terms of service: not researched — the 8-finding stop point was reached before this topic was reached.
- **One-sided termination rights**: not researched as a standalone topic (some overlap likely exists with the automatic-renewal and arbitration findings above, but no dedicated data was found).
- **Intellectual property (IP) assignment as its own measured problem**, separate from freelance payment terms: no dispute-count or survey number specifically isolating "client took my IP without paying" was found. The freelance non-payment data above is the closest available evidence, since many freelance disputes involve both non-payment and continued use of the freelancer's work.
- **A single, unified ranking of all clause types from one dataset**: does not exist in the sources reviewed. Every regulator and survey tracks its own clause type in isolation, so any cross-clause comparison — including the one in this report — is a synthesis across different studies, not one clean measurement.
- Several primary government source pages (ftc.gov, fedsmallbusiness.org, epi.org, federalregister.gov) blocked direct automated fetching (403 errors) during this research; the figures attributed to them here come from the search-result text of those same pages or from secondary sources that quote them directly, as noted next to each figure above.
