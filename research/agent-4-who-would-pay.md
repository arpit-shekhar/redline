# Agent 4 — Who would pay

## Method (searches run, pages read, when you stopped and why)

I ran 12 web searches (the maximum allowed) covering: general lawyer contract-review costs, lease review costs, freelance/Upwork/Fiverr lawyer pricing, small-business "can't afford a lawyer" complaints, LegalShield subscription pricing, terms-of-service reading habits, the Deloitte non-reading survey, influencer/brand-deal contract review, "just use ChatGPT instead of a lawyer" discussions, employment contract review costs, and a targeted Reddit search for "too expensive" complaints.

I then read (attempted to fetch) 15 individual pages to pull verbatim numbers and quotes. 8 of those fetches returned usable content; 7 were blocked by the site (HTTP 403 "Forbidden" or 429 "rate limited" — mostly Reddit, Quora, Upwork, G2, and one law-firm content page). Where a number or quote below came only from a search-engine summary rather than a page I could open directly, I've labeled it "via search summary" so you know it's one step less verified.

I stopped after hitting the 12-search cap and after collecting well more than the required 8 distinct, sourced findings — I did not go looking for more once I had solid coverage of segments, real prices, and evidence on both sides of the "will they pay" question.

## Segments, ranked by strength of evidence

**1. Small business owners and freelancers/independent contractors — strongest evidence.**
A real, currently-operating company called QwickContractReview.com sells exactly this kind of product — plain-English contract summaries, hidden-risk detection, red-flag identification — for a flat $99 (US dollars) per contract, turned around in 24–48 hours, marketed explicitly to "small businesses and freelancers" and "underserved entrepreneurs without access to costly legal services." Its founder is quoted saying: "Too many small businesses and freelancers sign contracts they don't fully understand — and end up paying the price later." Source: https://markets.financialcontent.com/clarkebroadcasting.mymotherlode/article/247pressrelease-2025-10-2-qwickcontractreviewcom-delivers-99-contract-reviews-in-48-hours-empowering-small-businesses-and-freelancers-nationwide
Separately, 62% of small business owners admitted they signed a contract without fully understanding it, according to a survey cited by i-agree.io. Source: https://i-agree.io/blog/do-your-clients-understand-your-contracts-e-sign-tools-wont-tell-you
LegalShield sells small-business legal plans at $49–$169 per month (US dollars) that bundle contract review with other legal help — real, ongoing subscription revenue for a similar need. Source: https://www.legalshield.com/business-plan/coverage-pricing

**2. Job candidates reviewing employment offers — solid evidence.**
Lawyers charge an average of about $430 (US dollars) as a flat fee to review an employment contract, per legal-marketplace data. Source: https://www.contractscounsel.com/b/employment-contract-review
On the pain side: 61% of employees said they signed a work contract without fully understanding it, and nearly one-third of that group later ran into a negative consequence at work because of it. Source: https://i-agree.io/blog/do-your-clients-understand-your-contracts-e-sign-tools-wont-tell-you

**3. Renters/tenants — solid but thinner evidence.**
A New York City tenant reported their lawyer wanted a $1,000 (US dollars) retainer just to read an unusually long lease, describing it as "SO EXTENSIVE, SO MANY PROVISIONS, AND PAGES TO THE EXTENT THAT THE TENANT'S LAWYER MUST SPEND NUMEROUS HOURS OR DAYS OF HIS TIME TO READ AND EVALUATE." Source: https://patch.com/new-york/foresthills/lease-resembles-the-bible
On a landlord/tenant forum, one attorney quoted 4–6 billable hours at $340/hour (up to roughly $2,000, US dollars) to review a lease, which at least one forum commenter called "outrageous" (via search summary — the forum page itself blocked direct reading). Source: https://www.biggerpockets.com/forums/52/topics/828474-how-much-to-pay-for-an-attorney-to-review-my-lease-agreement

**4. Creators/influencers reviewing brand deals — real but higher-priced, smaller pool.**
A law firm specializing in this exact niche, Kate Cooper Law, charges a flat $800 (up to 10 pages) to $1,400 (20+ pages) (US dollars) to review a brand-deal or management contract for a creator. Source: https://katecooperlaw.com/brand-deal-contract-review
This shows real willingness to pay at a high price point, but it's a narrower, higher-stakes segment (deals often involve intellectual-property rights and exclusivity clauses) rather than broad, everyday demand.

**5. Gig workers, landlords, and everyday consumers signing gym/telecom/subscription contracts — evidence not found.**
I did not find segment-specific pricing, complaints, or willingness-to-pay data for these groups within my search budget. See "What I could not find" below.

## What contract review costs today (real numbers only, each with a URL)

- General flat-fee contract review typically runs $250–$750 (US dollars); a simple NDA (a short confidentiality agreement) can be as low as $99–$350, while a complex partnership agreement can run up to $2,500. Source: https://mylegalpal.com/contract-review-cost-what-lawyers-actually-charge/ (aggregated across contractscounsel.com, upcounsel.com and similar sites in the search results)
- Hourly billing: solo lawyers $150–$250/hour, mid-size firm associates $250–$400/hour, large firm partners $400–$800+/hour (US dollars). Source: https://mylegalpal.com/contract-review-cost-what-lawyers-actually-charge/
- Employment contract review: averages $430 flat fee; a simple review can run $200–$500 (US dollars). Source: https://www.contractscounsel.com/b/employment-contract-review
- Freelance/service agreement review: $200–$500 flat fee (US dollars). Commercial lease review for a small business: $500–$1,500 flat fee (US dollars). Source: https://mylegalpal.com/contract-review-cost-what-lawyers-actually-charge/
- Brand-deal/influencer contract review: $800–$1,400 flat fee (US dollars), by pages. Source: https://katecooperlaw.com/brand-deal-contract-review
- Entry-level contract-law freelancers on Upwork charge $30–$150 per hour (US dollars) for standard contract review and minor edits (via search summary; the Upwork page itself blocked direct reading). Source: https://www.upwork.com/hire/contract-law-professionals/cost/
- LegalShield small-business legal plan: $49/month (Essential — 10 documents up to 15 pages reviewed per year), $99/month (Plus), $169/month (Pro), all in US dollars. Source: https://www.legalshield.com/business-plan/coverage-pricing
- QwickContractReview.com: $99 flat fee (US dollars) per single contract review, 24–48 hour turnaround — this is a live competitor product, not a lawyer. Source: https://markets.financialcontent.com/clarkebroadcasting.mymotherlode/article/247pressrelease-2025-10-2-qwickcontractreviewcom-delivers-99-contract-reviews-in-48-hours-empowering-small-businesses-and-freelancers-nationwide

## Evidence of willingness to pay (verbatim where possible + URLs)

- QwickContractReview.com is a real, operating business charging $99 (US dollars) per contract review, aimed squarely at freelancers and small businesses — its existence as an ongoing service is itself evidence that at least some people pay a two-figure-to-low-three-figure price for a "read my contract and tell me the risks in plain English" product, which is very close to part of the Redline idea. Source: https://markets.financialcontent.com/clarkebroadcasting.mymotherlode/article/247pressrelease-2025-10-2-qwickcontractreviewcom-delivers-99-contract-reviews-in-48-hours-empowering-small-businesses-and-freelancers-nationwide
- LegalShield has enough paying small-business subscribers to be a going concern at $49–$169/month, bundling contract review with other legal services — evidence people will pay monthly for on-demand legal help, though not proof they'd pay for a standalone contract-review app. Source: https://www.legalshield.com/business-plan/coverage-pricing
- Kate Cooper Law's creator clients pay $800–$1,400 per brand-deal review — real money changing hands for review of exactly the kind of document Redline targets, in the creator segment. Source: https://katecooperlaw.com/brand-deal-contract-review
- A working marketplace of contract-law freelancers exists on Upwork at $30–$150/hour, meaning people already search for and pay individuals to review contracts for them rather than doing it alone (via search summary). Source: https://www.upwork.com/hire/contract-law-professionals/cost/

## Evidence AGAINST willingness to pay (be blunt here)

- A 2017 Deloitte survey of 2,000 US consumers found 91% agree to legal terms and conditions without reading a single word of them; among 18–34 year-olds it's 97%. A huge share of people simply don't engage with contract review at all — free or paid — which undercuts the idea that they'd pay to have it explained to them. Source: https://i-agree.io/blog/do-your-clients-understand-your-contracts-e-sign-tools-wont-tell-you (citing the original Deloitte study)
- A University of Law (UK) study found 68% of people either don't read or don't understand the contracts they sign. Source: https://i-agree.io/blog/do-your-clients-understand-your-contracts-e-sign-tools-wont-tell-you
- An Adobe survey found 69% of consumers said they'd signed a contract despite not knowing all the details in it — and, tellingly, most of them signed anyway rather than stopping to get help. Source: https://i-agree.io/blog/do-your-clients-understand-your-contracts-e-sign-tools-wont-tell-you
- A May 2023 LexisNexis survey found only 27% of consumers said they'd even use a FREE general AI chat tool like ChatGPT for legal help. If roughly three out of four people won't use a free tool, that's a warning sign for a product that charges money to do something similar. Source: https://i-agree.io/blog/do-your-clients-understand-your-contracts-e-sign-tools-wont-tell-you
- Rocket Lawyer, an established legal-services brand, already offers "Rocket Copilot Contract Review" as a FREE AI tool for small business owners that highlights key terms and red flags — a free, well-known competitor already exists for the small-business segment, which is Redline's strongest segment above. Source: https://www.rocketlawyer.com/newsroom/rocket-lawyer-launches-rocket-copilot-contract-review
- General search results repeatedly surfaced other free or near-free AI contract tools (for example "LegalNow," described as a free AI legal assistant for small businesses) positioned directly against the same pain point — more evidence that "free chatbot" is a live alternative in this space, not a hypothetical one.

## What I could not find

- No verbatim forum or social-media post where someone specifically says "I would pay $X" for a contract/lease/terms-of-service explainer tool — I did not find one within the 12-search budget.
- No segment-specific pricing or complaint data for gig workers (e.g., Uber, DoorDash, Instacart independent-contractor agreements).
- No segment-specific data for landlords, or for consumers reviewing gym, telecom, or subscription contracts specifically (only general "people don't read terms and conditions" statistics, which are not specific to those industries).
- Could not independently verify the exact wording of the BiggerPockets "outrageous" comment — the forum page blocked automated reading, so that item rests on a search-engine summary rather than the primary source.
- No published bar-association fee survey (for example, from the American Bar Association) with contract-review-specific hourly rate data. The dollar figures in this report come from legal-marketplace and content-marketing sites (ContractsCounsel, UpCounsel, MyLegalPal) that make money by matching users to lawyers, so their published price ranges may skew toward numbers that make hiring a lawyer look either necessary or affordable, depending on the page's goal — treat them as industry-reported estimates, not independently audited data.
- Several potentially useful pages (Reddit threads, Quora, G2 reviews, Upwork's live pricing page) returned "403 Forbidden" or "429 rate limited" errors when I tried to read them directly, so quotes drawn from those sources came from search-engine snippets only, not a full read of the original page.
